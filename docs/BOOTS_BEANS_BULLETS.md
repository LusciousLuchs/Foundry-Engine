# Boots, Beans, and Bullets — Design Document

Status: **Draft 0.1**. Concept credit: the original idea is a friend's; this document turns it into a buildable plan.
Items marked **[PROPOSAL]** are my interpretation to fill gaps and need the concept owner's sign-off.
Items marked **[ASSUMPTION]** concern code that is not in this repository (see §9).

## 1. Pitch
A single-player, turn-based 3D strategy game played from a **command tent**. You never touch the battlefield: you read
maps, reports and scout intel, **predict what the enemy will do next**, and issue orders. If your read of the enemy is
imprecise, your troops pay for it. Win by out-thinking the opposing commander, not by out-clicking them.

## 1a. Setting and role (confirmed by the concept owner)
- **Era:** WW1-esque (trenches, field telephones, radio/wireless, artillery, early armour; fictional factions are fine).
- **Role:** a **field officer in the rear lines**. You are never at the front; everything you know arrives second-hand.
- **The tent is the base of operations** and has three core stations:
  1. **Radio** — intercept enemy transmissions. They are enciphered; you must **decipher** them to learn what the enemy plans.
  2. **Map** — soldiers at the front report spotted enemy movement; you plot it and **decide where to attack**.
  3. **Orders desk** — turn plans into orders to your units (see §3).
- Intelligence therefore comes from two independent, imperfect sources: **signals** (radio, cryptographic puzzle) and
  **observation** (spotter reports, plotted on the map). Cross-checking them is how you "predict with precision".

## 2. Pillars
1. **Prediction is the skill.** Every good turn starts with "what will they do?" and the game rewards the reading, not just the reaction.
2. **Command-tent fantasy.** The tent *is* the interface: map table, field radio, ration crate, wall of casualty tags.
3. **Consequences land on people.** Misjudgements cost named squads, supplies and morale, not an abstract score.
4. **Readable and fair.** Enemy behaviour follows learnable rules; no hidden dice that feel unearned.

## 3. Core loop (one turn)
1. **Briefing** — runners and field telephones bring spotter reports (movement seen at the front, with time and reliability);
   the radio operator hands you the turn's intercepts. Information is partial and ages.
2. **Analysis** — at the radio, decipher intercepts (§4a); at the map, plot spotter reports and look for patterns.
3. **Prediction** — on the map you mark expected enemy actions (move to / hold / attack / retreat / reinforce) with a confidence level.
4. **Orders** — assign each unit: move, hold, dig in, ambush, resupply, withdraw. Orders cost command points.
5. **Commit** — locks the plan. No take-backs.
6. **Resolution** — a 3D playback (the camera leaves the tent for a battlefield view) of both sides acting simultaneously; the camera leaves the tent for a battlefield view.
7. **After-action** — predictions are scored against what happened; casualties, morale and supply change; intel is updated.

## 4. Prediction system **[PROPOSAL]**
This is the heart of the game and the part most needing the concept owner's intent.
- **Predictions are explicit.** The player marks an enemy unit's likely action and a *certainty* (Hunch / Probable / Certain).
- **Precision pays and punishes.** A correct, confident prediction grants bonuses to units ordered against it (ambush bonus, free
  first strike, saved supplies). A wrong, confident prediction applies penalties to the *exposed* units: caught moving,
  flanked, out of ammo, morale shock. A "Hunch" is cheap and low-risk; "Certain" is high-risk/high-reward.
- **Not predicting is allowed** but plays blind: no bonuses, and default exposure to surprise.
- **Enemy AI is legible.** Each enemy officer has a doctrine (aggressive, cautious, supply-driven, ...) plus tells. The AI picks among
  candidate plans using utility scoring with seeded randomness, so a careful player can learn and exploit habits.
- **Intel quality** determines how many candidate enemy actions the UI can narrow down: better scouting = a shorter, more
  trustworthy list.

## 4a. Radio interception and deciphering (real cryptography, confirmed; details **[PROPOSAL]**)
- Each turn the radio picks up a few enemy messages on different frequencies. You tune the dial, record, then work on the text.
- **Real, historically plausible ciphers, solved by the player by hand** (no fake minigames): Caesar/shift, monoalphabetic
  substitution, keyword (Vigenère-style) polyalphabetic, columnar transposition, Playfair, and a ADFGX-style fractionated
  cipher late in the campaign. Messages are generated by actually encrypting plaintext, so every solution is genuine.
- **Daily key (core rule):** every in-game day the enemy uses a different key. **You must obtain that day's key before you can
  decipher that day's traffic.** Yesterday's key does not work today. Ways to get it:
  - **Capture:** raids on forward posts and dead couriers yield key sheets (usually for the *next* day or two, at a risk/cost).
  - **Key indicator in the message:** an indicator group points to a key in a **codebook you hold** (partly recovered over time).
  - **Cryptanalysis:** break the key from the ciphertext itself (frequency analysis, Kasiski/index of coincidence, known-plaintext
    "cribs" such as standard greetings, unit names and report formats). Slow, costs analysis actions, but needs no capture.
  - Keys follow a **rule you can learn** over a campaign (e.g. keyword derives from a place name in the previous day's report), so
    skilled players can anticipate keys. Rule changes are announced by intelligence.
- **Day boundary:** the key set rolls over at dawn. Unsolved messages from earlier days stay in a **backlog**, still decipherable
  with the right old key, but their intel ages and may be useless.
- **Tools on the desk:** cipher wheel, letter-frequency tally sheet, a codebook you partly recover from captured documents.
- **Partial success is meaningful:** a half-solved message gives fragments (a place name, a time, a unit number) so you can still
  use it, with lower reliability. Fully solved messages are marked reliable.
- **Time pressure through the turn structure, not a real-time timer:** you have limited **analysis actions** per turn (e.g. a
  radio operator and a clerk), so you choose which intercepts to work on.
- **Enemy can deceive:** decoy traffic, wrong-footing messages, radio silence before a real attack. Silence itself is intel.
- **Tutorial (required):** a guided, in-fiction training sequence run by a veteran signals sergeant. It introduces, in order:
  the radio and intercept log; what a cipher and a key are; Caesar shift with the cipher wheel; frequency analysis with the tally
  sheet; the daily key and why it matters; keyword ciphers; transposition; cribs; and using a deciphered message on the map.
  Each lesson is a short scripted puzzle with a checkable answer, can be replayed from a "Training Manual" prop in the tent, and
  later ciphers get a short refresher the first time they appear. First campaign missions use easy ciphers and generous keys.
- **Assists** (selectable difficulty): hint tiers, auto-built frequency tables, a "let the clerk try" option at a cost, and a
  scratch pad that checks letter-by-letter. Nothing is solved for you by default.

## 4b. Map and spotter reports **[PROPOSAL]**
- Front-line soldiers (observers, patrols, forward posts) send reports: *what* (infantry, guns, wagons), *where* (grid area, not
  exact), *when*, *how many (estimate)*, *heading*, and a **reliability** that depends on the observer's experience, light,
  weather and distance. Reports can be wrong or duplicated.
- You pin reports on the map; the map shows **fading markers** as reports age and a fog of unknown space.
- Corroboration rule: a radio message plus matching spotter reports produces a high-confidence enemy intention; conflicts
  force a judgement call.
- Where to attack is chosen on the map: pick objectives, assign forces, and see the predicted enemy response before committing.
- Your own observers can be moved, reinforced or lost; keeping eyes on the front is a resource decision.

## 5. Systems
| System | Summary |
|---|---|
| Map | Hex or square grid over a 3D terrain; elevation, cover, roads, fords, fog of war |
| Units | Squads (rifle, MG, mortar, scouts, medics, engineers, armour later). Strength, ammo, morale, fatigue |
| Supply ("Beans" and "Bullets") | Food and ammunition as two separate pools per unit; resupply routes can be cut |
| Morale ("Boots") | Fatigue and marching distance; exhausted units fight and move worse |
| Intel | Radio intercepts you decipher (§4a) and spotter reports you plot (§4b); later, aerial photos (delayed). Each item carries age and reliability |
| Weather/time | Rain, fog, night alter visibility, movement and what the enemy is likely to risk |
| Campaign | Sequence of scenarios with a persistent roster; casualties carry over, veterans improve |

Title tie-in: *Boots* = movement/fatigue, *Beans* = food/supply, *Bullets* = ammo/firepower. Resource tension is a core lever.

## 6. Presentation
- **Tent interior** (hub): map table with 3D terrain model and pinned report markers, **radio set** with tuning dial and
  headphones, cipher desk (wheel, frequency sheet, codebook), field telephone, ration crate, casualty list. Click a station to move the camera to it.
  All UI is diegetic where possible (paper reports, pins, grease-pencil on acetate).
- **Battlefield view** during resolution: camera orbits/follows action; units play move/fire/fall animations.
- **Style:** low-poly, warm lamp lighting inside, muted outdoor palette. Foundry has a sun, hemisphere ambient, 8 point lights
  and a sun shadow map — enough for lantern-lit tent interiors and daylight battlefields.
- **Audio:** canvas flapping, distant artillery, radio chatter, typewriter.

## 7. Technology
- **Engine:** Foundry Engine (this repo): C++, Vulkan 1.3, Windows x64 (runs on Linux via Wine/Proton), raylib-style compat API,
  Dear ImGui, scene files (`.fscene`), headless `fscene` command-line editor.
- **Game layout** (new repo or `games/bbb/`): 
  ```
  src/core/     rules engine: grid, units, orders, resolution, AI  (no rendering; unit-testable)
  src/app/      state machine: menu, tent, resolution, after-action, save/load
  src/ui/       ImGui panels + diegetic tent widgets
  assets/       models, textures, audio, scenarios (text/JSON)
  ```
- **Determinism:** resolution is a pure function of (state, orders, seed). Enables replays, tests, and "undo to briefing" for debugging.
- **Scenarios/data-driven:** units, doctrines and maps in text/JSON so content can be added without recompiling.
- **Physics:** not needed for rules (turn-based); only for cosmetic effects. Foundry's physics is on the roadmap and is not a dependency.
- **Known engine gaps to watch:** only 8 point lights; no cascaded shadows; built-in lit path transforms vertices on CPU (fine for
  low-poly scenes, watch unit counts); fullscreen is borderless; GPU testing so far is software-rasteriser only.

## 8. Update & installer **[ASSUMPTION]**
The brief: reuse the existing update and installer framework from Gunsmith3D. Observed in this repo
(`port/gunsmith3d/CMakeLists.txt`): the game links **winhttp** (consistent with an online update check), generates
`version.hpp` from a `version.hpp.in` with `MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH-<stage>` (e.g. `0.8.28-alpha`), and embeds `CHANGELOG.md` into the
binary. Plan:
1. Copy the Gunsmith3D updater/installer modules and their CMake/packaging scripts as-is; parameterise product name, app id,
   install directory, update-feed URL, executable name, icons and licence text. No behavioural changes in the first pass.
2. Keep the same version scheme and release stages so the existing release tooling applies.
3. Keep the in-game changelog view fed from `CHANGELOG.md`.
4. Use a **separate update feed and app id** so Gunsmith3D and this game can never update or uninstall each other.
5. Acceptance: fresh install, update from N-1 to N, uninstall, and run under Wine all succeed.

**Blocker:** that code lives in the Gunsmith3D repository, which this session cannot see. Before implementation I need the repo
attached (or the updater/installer directories pasted) to confirm module boundaries and what must be parameterised.

## 10. Risks
- **Prediction UX:** if marking predictions feels like chores, the pillar fails. Prototype early (M1/M3) with paper-like quick UI.
- **AI feels random or psychic:** mitigate with doctrines, visible tells, seeded determinism, and tuning tools.
- **Cryptography difficulty:** real ciphers can wall off players. Mitigate with the tutorial, assists, generous early keys, and playtesting with non-cryptographers.
- **Scope:** 3D tent + battlefield + campaign is a lot. Rules-first order (M1 before art) keeps it shippable.
- **Engine maturity:** real-GPU behaviour untested; schedule hardware testing before M4.
